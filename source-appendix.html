Zhao Shukai: China's “National Conditions” Are No Argument Against Reform or Enlightenment

“National conditions” is one of the most durable phrases in Chinese political debate. During the failed reforms of 1898, reformers often felt compelled to present railways, women’s education and representative government as ideas already sanctioned by Confucianism — the ideological foundation of the imperial order. Nearly a century later, some Chinese leaders warned that a market economy was incompatible with the country’s conditions and would bring chaos. In both cases, the existing political order was portrayed as a natural outgrowth of China’s enduring national conditions, rooted in its culture, history, demography, and other distinctive circumstances. History, however, proved far less fixed. Within a generation, intellectuals were challenging the authority of the classics; decades later, market reforms would transform China. In the follwing essay, Zhao Shukai argues that no set of national conditions lasts forever, because the people who shape those conditions do not stand still. He calls for an enlightenment aimed not at teaching citizens, especially farmers, to comply with government directives and obey the Party and political leaders, but at enabling them to think independently, understand their rights, participate in public affairs, and hold those in power accountable.
