The Chinese Invention of Reactionary Thought

Political debate in 9th century China was incentivized by the rejection of one specific tradition, under which crown princes visited ancestral temples when they were installed as heirs apparent. This practice was replaced by new rituals as late Tang Dynasty emperors started to leave the throne not to their eldest sons but, at least in theory, the best man in the empire – who almost always happened to be one of their eldest sons, all the same. The shift may appear to be minor, even meaningless, but the new ritual minimized the importance of heredity, while increasing that of merit, which opened up some space for scholars, at least in theory, to discuss the merits that would be desirable in rulers. Importantly, this approach didn’t mean that rulers were perceived as any less celestial than before. On the contrary, the addition of selection by virtue to the Mandate of Heaven actually made rulers, and even their appointees, more celestial than ever before. The visiting Japanese monk Ennin describes a magistrate’s Another factor fueling debate in the late Tang was the traditional assumption that administrative competence was never enough for officials – and, by extension, even emperors subjected to the Mandate of Heaven, although this was rarely spelled out. The basic Chinese concept was that all matters of government had to be informed with Confucian moral awareness, lest government deteriorate into disorder and tyranny, so discussion to pinpoint the exact degree of moral awareness of specific government acts had to be allowed. Much of this discussion was rather opaque and esoteric, to avoid political backlash, and conducted via commentaries and textual selection. The great scholar Yan Shigu (581-645) had been commissioned early in the dynasty to establish definite texts of the Five Confucian Classics, and this led to a flurry of production of new commentaries on the classics: debate over the official interpretation was encouraged, to reconcile and harmonize the different approaches to the textual tradition that had emerged during the Northern and Southern Dynasties. The historian Liu Zhiji (661-721) did much to establish rules for the debating game with his “Shitong,” the first Chinese work of historiography, discussing various approaches to historical compilation. The fact that the Tang had a surplus of poets and wannabe writers, to the point that Chinese fiction can be said to have been invented during this era, only contributed to an animated environment for politically-charged discussion. This trend was already evident in the work of the poet Chen Ziang (661-702), who developed his rejection of the contemporary world into a major poetic theme and pinned for what he himself described as a return to antique mores. Chen’s approach was hugely influential particularly from the second half of the 8th century, to the point that he may be considered the first example of reactionary thinker in Chinese and even world history. The reactionary lamentation for a world lost wasn’t anything new. Many Graeco-Roman writers had reflected before on how specific times and places, specific peoples, were superior to their contemporaries. The new element in Chen’s writing, one that was also included in the works of many scholars of the late 8th century and 9th century, was the addition of a call for action: a call for a return to better times and places, for the reshaping of modern people so that it would look more like old people.
