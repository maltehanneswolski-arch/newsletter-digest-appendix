I’ve written extensively about the fate of antiquity’s city-states in Greece and Italy. Most went through a cycle of monarchy, oligarchy and democracy, and the one that eventually won the game was Rome, which never quite reached the democratic stage — Augustus made sure of that — but ended up as a sort of populist polis ruling over a huge chunk of territory: what we call the Roman Empire. One of the reasons why Greek city-states couldn’t outcompete Hellenistic monarchies first and Rome later is that democratic systems across the Greek became chaotic and unruly, as elites dominated elections through massive bribery and control of propaganda. Concerns about such dominance led to the widespread use of sortition instead of election systems, that is, the casting of lots to select candidates for political office. I discussed this issue here, for example: For a long time, there have been reasons to suspect the Romans also used sortition to pick officials. Now, thanks to this new discovery in Spain, we know for certain. The paper is based on a restored copper vessel from Cartagena found in a collapsed room sealed by fire in Cartagena’s Molinete Archaeological Park, where excavations of the Atrium Building uncovered a copper-alloy sitella crushed beneath debris from an upper floor. The fire that destroyed the structure around the year 300 AD carbonized the wooden beams, collapsed the ceiling and protected the vessel from further corrosion. Despite being reduced to more than 250 fragments, the hammered copper sitella preserved an inscription with the name of a Roman official, Spurius Lucretius Tricipitinus, and the word “sortes,” referring to the wooden or metal tablets used in Roman lot-casting. This is pretty solid, substantial evidence for the practice. And very early evidence as well, since the piece is older than the structure destroyed, and probably dates back to the 1st century BC and the late Republican era. Seems like the Republican Romans, like the Greeks before them, were getting sick of electing people and started to draw lots instead. Funny how people over time keep getting disappointed by electoral cycles.
