How China & the Abbasid Caliphate Went Down Together (II)

The fundamental issue that both China and the Abbasid Caliphate faced was very similar to that faced by the Eastern Han Dynasty six centuries earlier: even in the absence of great military pressures on the borders, both states had grown too large to allow for easy communications between the center and the peripheries. Even the Romans, masters of transportation and civil engineering who were helped by the fact that many intra-empire communications were facilitated by the Mediterranean Sea, had found during the 2nd and 3rd centuries that their own empire often was unwieldy and some corners too remote, which allowed for the growth of local elites isolated from the center. The Caliphate and China, not blessed with anything resembling the superb road networks built by the Romans, struggled even more to maintain regular contact with regions that just became accustomed to governing themselves, like the eastern Iranian area of Khorasan and the zonas around the Bohai Sea. Local historians, often employed by the bureaucracy, were prone to castigating emperors for their lack of attention, judging that such laxity was a key driver in the rise of entrenched regional aristocracies. The truth, however, is that both the Caliphate and China had dynamic rulers who strove to maintain their predominance. Chinese Emperor Dezong displayed much strategic and tactic ability by securing an alliance with the state of Nanzhao, and later leading an invasion into Tibet that effectively ended with five decades of Tibetan raids across China’s western borders.
