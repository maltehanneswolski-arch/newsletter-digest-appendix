The Shia-Sunni Split

The uprising that gave way to the Abbasid Caliphate was launched from a land long settled and influenced by Iranians, and to some degree inspired by Shia ideas then spreading among Iranian-speaking Muslims. The Shias, fueled by their remembrance of their martyred candidate to lead the believers, Ali, insisted on kinship, holding that their imams had to be drawn from the Prophet’s family. Since Muhammad had left no male heirs, and Semitic tradition gave descendance through the female line much less weight, in practice this put the focus on candidates from the clan of Muhammad’s 5th century ancestor Hashim (the Hasimiyya), including descendants of the purported Abbasid ancestor Abbas. Most dangerous for the Abbasids were the Shias who preferred the line that ran from Husayn all the way to his descendant Musa al-Kazim (745-799). All this may sound like a contest over names, but it went well beyond that: the struggle that put the Abbasids atop the Islamic world was one that would shape the future of much of Eurasia and Africa. Musa Al-Kazim spent much of his life in Abbasid prisons, and most of the rest intriguing against the Abbasids. Eventually, Al-Kazim would become the seventh imam in Twelver Shia Islam, the largest branch of Shia ever since the split with the Sunnis emerged; although that only happened when these Twelvers organized as a politico-religious movement starting in the late 9th century – following the death of Hasan al-Askari, believed to be Al-Kazim’s descendant, in 874. On the other hand, the Abbasids derived support from the Kaysanites, a Shia sect heavily tainted by Zoroastrian beliefs and well entrenched among Iranian mawali. This sect in particular defended Hanafiyya’s line, and its initial support of the Abbasids was instrumental for the Abbasid revolution – since the Abbasids claimed that they inherited the imamate from a descendant of Hanafiyya himself, according to his last will and testament. This was highly convenient for the Abbasids: insofar as early Shias had a political program, it lay in rebellion for the sake of restoring effective political rule to the family of the Prophet – the rest would take care of itself. The US and China should be talking about AI safety and governance, and while I am skeptical this can happen given the politics I thought it is still constructive to make some suggestions. Bill Executive Summary The United States and the People's Republic of China should establish a Strategic AI Dialogue (SAID): a permanent, senior-level government-to-government mechanism focused on managing the most consequential risks arising from increasingly capable artificial intelligence. SAID should be led on the U.S. side by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and on the PRC side by Vice Premier He Lifeng. The White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP), led by Michael Kratsios, should be a standing senior participant on the U.S. side and should coordinate much of the scientific and technical work on frontier models, AI evaluation, biotechnology, standards, and AI-enabled scientific research. Their involvement would give the process sufficient political and technical weight while connecting AI risks to the broader economic and strategic relationship. SAID should meet quarterly in a neutral country, preferably Switzerland, with working groups operating continuously between meetings. Its objective would not be to resolve the larger technological competition between the United States and China, nor should cooperation require either side to compromise legitimate national-security, economic, or technological interests. Instead, SAID should concentrate on a narrower proposition: there are AI risks for which the United States and China are mutually vulnerable, cannot fully protect themselves acting alone, and have a shared interest in preventing even while remaining strategic competitors. The initial agenda should emphasize nuclear command and control, catastrophic biological risks including mirror life, frontier-model safety, AI incident notification, financial stability, employment disruption, cyber risks, AI-enabled crime, and other areas where limited but meaningful cooperation is realistic. 1.
