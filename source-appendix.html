. Now is the time for Washington to show resolve and deter another global maritime crisis. learn more. Expect A More Dangerous Asia If Maritime Issues Don’t Make the Trump-Xi Agenda. By Harrison Prétat . Things are not working the way they are supposed to in the Strait of Hormuz—and, increasingly, neither are they in the waters of Asia. Traffic has been throttled in the Strait of Hormuz since the beginning of the Iran conflict in March, sending energy prices sky-high and creating rippling economic uncertainty that has threatened the health of the global economy. Fears of a similar situation emerging in Asia have never felt as real: In the last year, amid U.S. preoccupation in the Western Hemisphere and Iran, China has made unprecedented strides in eroding the navigational and economic rights of neighboring states in pursuit of maximal control of its near seas. Its latest move, interfering with commercial traffic east of Taiwan with a new, persistent coast guard presence , has sharply raised fears of a maritime blockage in Asia that would have even more massive impacts than the closure of Hormuz. Capabilities… . Neither of these problems have easy—or purely military—answers. U.S. forces have tried to secure the Strait of Hormuz through mine-clearing operations and strikes on Iranian capabilities threatening commercial traffic. But despite these efforts, the United States’ overwhelming military superiority has not translated into an ability to restore traffic through the strait. Even a minimum Iranian capability to strike transiting ships has been enough to deter most commercial ships or convince them to proceed on Iran’s terms—along new routes and paying tolls. In Asia, the U.S. tool kit for addressing China’s maritime expansionism has also relied heavily on its military. The U.S. Navy has long conducted freedom of navigation operations to challenge China’s (and others’) excessive maritime claims.
