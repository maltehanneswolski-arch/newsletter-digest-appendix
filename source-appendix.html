The Holodomor (I): How the Ukraine Became a Nation

The Bolsheviks who won the Russian Civil War in the early 1920s saw Russian nationalism as the number one threat to their new Soviet state. In Soviet parlance, Russian nationalism was called “Great Russian chauvinism,” and Lenin’s argument at the time was that Russified natives — unlike himself, who was a Russian-language native speaker — were often the worst chauvinists. In a flight of inspiration, Lenin coined the term “rusotiapstvo” (mindless Russian chauvinism), which then entered the Bolshevik lexicon and became an invaluable weapon in the rhetorical arsenals of the national republics. Soon before his death in 1924, Lenin wrote a memorandum in which he stated his fears for the future. And he, again, focused such fears on Russian nationalism: after the suppression of the right of determination within the Soviet Union, he explained, minority nationalities were in danger of being delivered up to “this 100% Russian phenomenon, Great Russian chauvinism, which is characteristic of the Russian bureaucracy.” The practical results of these were multiple and targeted attacks against ethnic Russians: they had their culture stigmatized as oppressive and suffered discrimination throughout the history of the Soviet Union, at first obvious and openly, and later, from the mid- to late-1930s, discreetly. Every Soviet republic was given its own Communist Party but the Russian Federation, where the Soviet Communist Party (originally called “Russian”) had been created, would have to do without one. The Russian Nikolai Bukharin, an old Bolshevik, put it bluntly during the Twelfth Congress of the Communist Party, from April 17 to April 25, 1923: “as the former Great Power nation, we should indulge the nationalist aspirations (of the non-Russians) and place ourselves in an unequal position.” Bukharin himself, of course, was not in any unequal position: he meant other people should take that uncomfortable position. As a result, the Soviet Union was the first of the world’s old multiethnic states to promote the national consciousness of its ethnic minorities and establish for them many of the characteristic institutional forms of the nation-state. That example that would be followed by Communists and progressives in places like China and South America even before the fashion swept Western Europe, including the likes of Spain and the United Kingdom, particularly from the late 20th century. To further such goals, the Soviet state created a dozen large national republics, as well as tens of thousands of national territories scattered across the entire expanse of the Soviet Union. New national elites were trained and promoted. In each territory, the national language was declared the official language of government, even when – as in dozens of cases – this necessitated the creation of a written language where one did not yet exist. The Soviet state financed the mass production of books, journals, newspapers, movies, operas, museums, folk music ensembles and other cultural output in the non-Russian languages. Nothing comparable had been attempted before.
