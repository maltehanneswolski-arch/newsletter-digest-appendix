Policy Currents. The newsletter for policy people Web version: ** Aug. 12, 2026 Mirror Life Could Be Catastrophic. Here's How to Prevent It Mirror life does not yet exist. Humanity should hope it stays that way. This hypothetical form of life, built from biomolecules with the opposite molecular orientation of those found in nature, could emerge within the next few decades. If that happens, then the consequences could be catastrophic for human health and natural ecosystems. A new RAND report lays out a strategy for how the United States can stop mirror life before it starts. What exactly is mirror life? The building blocks of life--DNA, RNA, and proteins--each have a specific handedness. In nature, DNA and RNA are right-handed, while proteins are left-handed. But scientists can reverse that handedness and create mirror-image molecules in the lab. Mirror life would be built entirely from such molecules. Why does it matter? Immune systems, predators, and most medicines are tuned to natural-handed biology. That means mirror bacteria could prove evasive, spreading through ecosystems and causing irreversible harm. What can be done? The risks posed by mirror life must be prevented entirely, not merely mitigated. That's because the first successful creation of mirror life could mark the point at which defense becomes impossible. Our report outlines several steps the United States can take now to anticipate this threat. These include engaging other major scientific powers (especially China), promoting restraint within the scientific community, and making a clear commitment to not pursue mirror life, even if others are suspected of doing so. These and other recommendations from the study, which is part of a broader RAND effort focused on mirror life, are key to understanding this unprecedented challenge. The authors' message is clear: The only reliable defense against mirror life is prevention, so strategic planning should begin now. . Evaluating Pathways for U.S. Shipbuilding Cooperation with Allies. Report by Henry H. Carroll , Cynthia R. Cook , Seamus P. Daniels , and Oliver B. Buntin. The U.S. naval shipbuilding enterprise continues to face workforce, infrastructure, supply chain, and procurement constraints that limit its ability to deliver ships at the pace, scale, and cost the Navy requires. While allied cooperation is not a panacea for the United States' shipbuilding challenge, this report examines how deeper cooperation with allies—with a specific focus on South Korea and Japan—might realistically contribute to the United States overcoming some of these constraints. It considers four principal pathways: (1) allied investment in U.S. shipyards, (2) modular or “green hull” coproduction, (3) procurement of allied-built vessels, and (4) expanded overseas maintenance and repair. No single pathway advances every U.S. objective. Hybrid approaches, however, could balance competing priorities by pairing near-term foreign construction with investment in U.S. shipyards, workforce development, technology transfer, and the phased expansion of domestic participation. Such models offer a method to balance the tradeoffs among speed, affordability, and long-term U.S. industrial capacity.
