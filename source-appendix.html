By the early 1930s, non-Russian nationalism in the Soviet republics — including Ukrainian nationalism — for the first time became the target of attacks similar to those long directed against the Russian version. This drive against regional empowerment was part of a large-scale turn towards collectivism and enhanced control of the economy, also involving the establishment of an internal passport system in 1932. Behind the drive, there was a growing belief within the Soviet leadership that the country was ready for an additional nudge towards full industrialization and collectivization. This belief, like so many within the Soviet leadership, was entirely wrong. Then again, there were reasons for optimism in the Soviet Union around 1930. With the Siberian goldfields yielding ready capital, great new electric utilities and mining combines dotted the landscape of eastern Ukraine, while gigantic tractor and auto factories dominated the industrial suburbs of Moscow, Leningrad, and Chelyabinsk. Magnitogorsk, a planned industrial city near Orenburg, housed the largest iron- and steelworks in the world, employing nearly a quarter of a million people. Yekaterinburg in the Ural Mountains, where Tsar Nicholas II and his family were murdered in July 1918, was now better known for housing Uralmash, the world’s largest complex for the construction of heavy machinery. Collaboration with sympathetic, progressive American industrialists had produced great fruits in the shape of huge iron and steel combines in Leningrad – as Petrograd was renamed in 1924 – overseen by the Freyn Engineering Company of Chicago, Illinois, hired in May 1927. Magnitogorsk itself was designed from top to bottom by Arthur G. Mc Kee and Company of Cleveland, Ohio, based on the prototype of a US Steel plant in Gary, Indiana. American experts from the Mac Donald Engineering Company of Chicago had overseen the construction of four of the world’s largest cement combines, one at Kerch on the Black Sea, two outside Moscow, and another just east of the Ural Mountains. The international scenario was also favorable.
