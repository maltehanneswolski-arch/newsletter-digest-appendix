How China & the Abbasid Caliphate Went Down Together (I)

By the 8th century, the rising influence of Turkic mercenaries and warlords was ominous both in the Abbasid Caliphate and the Chinese Empire. The Turks were employed to paper over the cracks for armies lacking sufficient numbers of reliable, warlike locals, and became elite forces with little affection or respect for their rulers. After the rebellion of An Lushan — one such Turk — the desperate Chinese paid huge sums to the Uyghur Turks for poor quality horses and not overly reliable mercenaries, and Uyghur merchants secured extraterritorial rights and privileges and were allowed to settle in Chang’an, together with Sogdian colleagues. Much the same happened in the Caliphate, particularly after Samarra was founded in 836 and temporarily became the capital. Turkish forces there – composed of a mixture of mercenary warbands and “ghilman” slaves captured in the battlefield, who were slaves only in the loosest possible sense of the word[1] [ # ftn1 ] – became highly influential as one of the most significant powers behind the throne. Given these developments, ethnic tensions were another common feature of both empires in this era.
