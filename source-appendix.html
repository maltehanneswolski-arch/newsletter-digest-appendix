Bede & the End of the Dark Ages

Bede (672-735), abbot of the monastery of Jarrow and inheritor of the classicist Christian tradition preserved in Ireland thanks to Patrick’s efforts, was like Gregory of Tours and Isidore of Seville almost certainly descended, at least in part, from the old Romano-British elite. Bede’s role was similar to them, in that as he rose to become the land’s most prominent priest he was expected to behave as a Roman curial or senator: both an extension of and a controller of the secular government, fulfilling the role that the Christian Church had had in the Roman Empire first since the 4th century, and later in the successor barbarian kingdoms. This role entailed a lot of political maneuvering, and Bede displayed great skill at that game. Living in an era in which Mercia – Northumbria’s main enemy – remained Britain most powerful state despite several political crises, he wrote with remarkable hostility towards Mercian rulers and aristocrats, in a tone that is redolent of that adopted by Gregory of Tours when discussing the Merovingian brutes and pagans or semi-pagans he had had to endure in Gaul a century earlier. His usefulness as defender of Northumbrian independence explains Bede’s ability to write rather freely. He did much with that freedom, becoming a reformer of British institutions: he helped popularize the practice of using Anni Domini to date calendars from the supposed date of Jesus Christ’s birth, and fomented the teaching of classical Latin and Greek among a population that had quickly grown disconnected from its Roman past. Bede wasn’t a mere nostalgic, however. That he was cognizant of living in a completely new era with a completely new social, mental and even ethnic framework is evident in the title of his most famous work, “Historia Ecclesiastica Gentis Anglorum.” This is the first book in which the term “English people” is used to describe not just the dominant subset of Germanic invaders/settlers that moved into Britain, but the population of mixed parentage speaking the dominant language in the land that by his time was already commonly named after those invaders/settlers. The Historia Ecclesiastica is a founding work of English historiography, being the only source for many of the things Bede reports. Filled with miracles and displaying a reverence for authority that would have been embarrassing for Graeco-Roman historians, and even quite a few Christian theologians, it contains the story of Drycthelm, a man who briefly died and, before he was resuscitated, was given a tour of the afterlife. The tour included a long detour through a purgatory with fire and ice storms – “you’re not in hell yet,” an angel told him – as well as assemblies with people dressed in white – “you’re not in heaven yet,” the angel said. Bede, while telling the story, interspersed a verse from the Aeneid, for good measure.
