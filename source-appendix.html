Charlemagne Vs the Pagans

During campaigns in Italy and Saxony, Charlemagne did much to promote the Christianization of remaining pagan Germans, by forcing them into the faith at sword-point. And it indeed was in Germany in 777, at a large-scale assembly in Paderborn with Frankish and Saxon nobles, that Charlemagne met with dissident Muslims in command of northern Spanish cities. These Muslims secured his help against Abd al-Rahman, the Umayyad who had taken control of Cordoba. A short but momentous campaign followed, in which Frankish forces crossed the Pyrenees divided in two armies, one crossing the western passes across Basque lands, the other the eastern passes into what later would be called Catalonia. Both armies met with remarkable success, by advancing through regions tenuously held, if at all, by small Muslim garrisons: both the Basques and the cities of the former Roman province of Tarraconensis had mostly surrendered to the Muslims under jizya agreements that had entailed little destruction and left them considerable autonomy, so the arrival of the Franks incentivized them to renounce such deals, in exchange for vassalage to their new Christian overlord. They put the city under a siege that represented a mortal danger for the Al-Andalus emirate: if Zaragoza were to fall, the Muslims had no strongholds of comparable size to defend for hundreds of kilometers, so they would lose the entire Tarraconensis if not more territory. In the event, the Frankish armies abandoned the siege after a mere month. Zaragoza would remain Muslim for over three centuries and, when it was finally reconquered by Spanish armies, its fall did lead to the collapse of the Muslim position in eastern Spain. Still, Charlemagne’s triumphal return to his lands north of the Pyrenees left an important fact on the ground behind: most of the Christian rulers who supported Charlemagne’s campaigns eventually coalesced in what the Frankish king called “Spanish March,” a loose Christian coalition in control of lands south and east of the Pyrenees that, with the Frankish occupation of Barcelona in 801, became a formal territory under loose Frankish rule, fully independent from the Muslim emirate. On the western side of the Pyrenees, the Franks weren’t received quite as enthusiastically. On his way back from Zaragoza, Charlemagne had the walls of Pamplona, the largest Basque town at the time, torn down so that the notoriously unreliable Basques couldn’t use it to house Muslim garrisons. As a response, Basque tribesmen ambushed the departing Frankish army in the so-called Battle of Roncesvalles, leading to significant, if not critical, Frankish losses.
