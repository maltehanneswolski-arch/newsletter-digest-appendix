“Peace”

View this post on the web at https://anneapplebaum.substack.com/p/peace

Young demonstrators across Europe, many with legitimate grievances about jobs and housing, have unexepectedly begun talking about “peace” in the past few weeks. Protestors in Italy seemed to lump together wars in the Middle East and Ukraine, and are calling on their government to stop spending money on weapons [ https://substack.com/redirect/1eb222f5-89b9-4d49-9465-f12eb2de8871?j=eyJ1IjoiNnhha3A0In0.Jik053lvvE-Ofpv9cpN9G_-RUPbW_JKjUogath2oHFk ], including in Ukraine. Politicians have used the same narrative. In Bulgaria, the prime minister has also called for a halt in aid to Ukraine; the issue has become part of the Bulgarian presidential campaign. Both Alice Weidel, leader of the far-right Alliance fur Deutschland (AfD) and Marine LePen, leader of the French far-right, have made similar arguments: stop sending weapons so that there will be “peace.” I understand why this simple formula is appealing. But it is also misleading. Those using those slogans are not asking what would happen if Europeans really did stop sending money and weapons to Ukraine. Since January of 2025, the US has not made any new material contribution to the war. Thanks entirely to support from Europe and others, including Canada, Australia and Japan, Ukraine has defended itself anyway. Ukrainians are incredibly innovative, and have they built the world’s highest-tech drone industry. But without funding, sooner or later, the Ukrainian frontline would break. And then what? One thing is certain: the fighting would not end, and violence might grow worse. Ukraine would collapse into guerilla war. Europe would have a lawless zone, an Afghanistan, on its borders. Russia would inflict the same kind of repression on Kyiv and central Ukraine that it has applied in occupied western Ukraine, murdering leaders, setting up concentration camps, building a police state, kidnapping children. Millions of refugees would stream into Europe to escape the moden equivalent of a Nazi occupation. The Russian army, after capturing Ukrainian resources and maybe even the Ukrainian defense industry, would be reinvigorated, and ready to move farther into more countries that Putin considers to be part of his empire. Would this be “peace”? Would Europeans be able to stop spending money on weapons? No. We would face an uglier, more frightening war, one that would require Europeans to double or triple their military spending. Instead of spending budgets on health care or education, Europeans would have to completely re-imagine their security. Without the Ukrainian army protecting Europe from Russia, Europeans would have to change their way of life entirely. Peace will come someday, but not from cutting support for Ukraine. The war will end when Putin gives up his dream of absorbing Ukraine into Russia, when he understands that he cannot win. Tragically, the Trump administration has fed his dream, and prolonged the war by negotiating business deals that encourage Putin to keep fighting. But there are limits to what Russia can absorb, and how many lives they can throw away. The Midterm Conversation We’re Avoiding

View this post on the web at https://crookedmedia.substack.com/p/the-midterm-conversation-were-avoiding

“How are you feeling about the election?” is the question I get from pretty much everyone I talk to this time of year. People who don’t know me as well usually follow up with some version of “sorry, you must get tired of talking about this.” But I don’t! I honestly love talking about politics with anyone, anytime for the same reason I do it professionally: I’m a sicko. I’ll be honest, though: outside of work, I’m less comfortable having conversations with people about their politics, especially if they differ from mine. Some of that is a congenital aversion to conflict that I promise I’m working on. But I feel like these conversations have become even harder. And I’m not the only one. Fifty-six percent of Americans have stopped discussing political news with someone because of something that person said, according to Pew. That’s up from 45% in 2024. Look, I get it. Speculating with someone about what other voters will do on Tuesday is easier than asking what they’re gonna do and why – especially if they give you an opinion you disagree with, or tell you they aren’t convinced voting is even worth it. At which point you might change the subject because you’d rather not pick a fight or just don’t know what to say next. The internet and social media have given us countless ways to express our opinions while avoiding conversations that ask more of us. We can post our takes (guilty), fight with strangers (guilty), share things we agree with and dunk on things we don’t (guilty and guilty). And we can do it all without ever having to really understand someone else’s frustration, or respond to criticism we find unfair, or actually try to persuade people who might need more than a link you posted with an all-caps THIS and multiple clap emojis. Campaigns have a related problem: they’ve gotten much better at figuring out which voters they want to reach, but much worse at actually reaching them. In 2024, Democrats made 300 million attempts to contact voters by phone. They got through just 3% of the time. And honestly, my first reaction was: who are those 3%? I barely answer calls from people I know. Same with texts and emails. That’s why we have spam filters and Ring cameras. We’ve spent years developing increasingly sophisticated ways to avoid strangers who want something from us – while campaigns have spent that time developing increasingly sophisticated ways to become the strangers we avoid. But what if the answer isn’t finding a better way for strangers to reach us?...

Who’s Afraid of “ByteDance Time”?

View this post on the web at https://www.pekingnology.com/p/whos-afraid-of-bytedance-time

In almost any other industry, capturing the bulk of your customers’ waking hours is the ultimate compliment. In China’s fiercely competitive, hyper-scrutinised tech sector, it sounds more like a threat. When a prominent commentator recently heralded the arrival of “ByteDance Time” on the Chinese internet, the ensuing public squabble revealed exactly why a tech giant might violently reject its own coronation. The phrase was coined late last month by Qin Shuo 秦硕, a veteran business commentator, in an essay [ https://substack.com/redirect/df61ad1c-4d49-411c-a6fb-a338db715aaf?j=eyJ1IjoiNnhha3A0In0.Jik053lvvE-Ofpv9cpN9G_-RUPbW_JKjUogath2oHFk ] examining Hongguo, ByteDance’s phenomenally successful micro-drama app. Citing a Nomura report based on QuestMobile data, Mr Qin noted that ByteDance apps swallowed 40.9% of the time spent on China’s 50 most-used mobile apps in July, compared to Tencent’s 29.1%. Hongguo alone attracted 168m daily active users—more than China’s four leading traditional streaming apps combined. To Mr Qin, the data posed a provocative question: has the Chinese internet shifted from an era anchored by Tencent’s social networks to one ruled by ByteDance’s algorithmic entertainment? It is a staggering commercial feat. Few companies have ever translated consumer behaviour into frictionless code so successfully. From Toutiao and Douyin to TikTok and AI assistants, ByteDance’s ascent is a masterclass in engineering and corporate agility. Yet Mr Qin’s awe was laced with dread. He fretted that Hongguo’s cliffhanger-driven dopamine loop would inevitably cannibalise the time people once reserved for reading, reflection, and family life. ByteDance went on the offensive. Li Liang 李亮, a senior communications executive at the firm, dismissed the “ByteDance Time” label as “complete nonsense.” He argued the Nomura data was narrow and noted that competitive leads are notoriously fragile. The dispute quickly devolved from methodology to mudslinging. Pointedly noting Mr Qin’s recent appearance at a Tencent creators’ event, and his past work editing books authorised by ByteDance’s arch-rival, Mr Li planted a seed of suspicion regarding the commentator’s allegiances. Mr Qin flatly rejected the insinuations. Yet in the paranoid corridors of Chinese tech, a well-placed insinuation often does the heavy lifting. The ferocity of the exchange begs a deeper question: why would a company fight so hard to deny its own dominance? The answer lies, at least partly, in the unforgiving nature of Chinese tech regulation. In a jurisdiction where powerful agencies exercise sweeping, often unchallengeable authority over internet platforms, anti-monopoly battles are frequently fought and decided in the court of public opinion long before any official paperwork is filed. For a Chinese tech giant, allowing a narrative of market dominance to take hold can carry significant risks. Once a regulator acts on a widely accepted public narrative, reversing that momentum is nearly impossible. A label like “ByteDance Time” is thus less a badge of honour than a source of unwelcome regulatory attention. This peril is compounded by China’s expectation for its digital champions to drive industrial upgrading and fundamental scientific breakthroughs, rather than merely perfecting the monetization of leisure. These anxieties recently crystallised when a provocative comparison went viral online recently, claiming that while America directs the bulk of its AI computing power towards coding and enterprise research, China squanders its AI resources on generating cheap revenge-fantasy micro-dramas. The People’s Daily, the Communist Party of China’s flagship newspaper, rushed to debunk [ https://substack.com/redirect/c5ddaebc-b67c-4e37-818c-4abf95b22e44?j=eyJ1IjoiNnhha3A0In0.Jik053lvvE-Ofpv9cpN9G_-RUPbW_JKjUogath2oHFk ] the statistics as flawed and defended the value of entertainment while stressing China’s investments in hard tech. But the lament that “America uses gunpowder to make weapons; China uses it to make fireworks [ https://substack.com/redirect/1d53f81d-392a-4dce-b0da-bd1dececa924?j=eyJ1IjoiNnhha3A0In0.Jik053lvvE-Ofpv9cpN9G_-RUPbW_JKjUogath2oHFk ]” struck a chord. It exposed a simmering national anxiety: what good is world-class algorithmic prowess if it is solely deployed to build a better entertainment house? But should a company be penalized simply for being wildly successful at entertaining the masses? Ming Jinwei, a former state-media journalist writing under the influential moniker Mingshu Zatan, leapt to ByteDance’s defence. In an essay titled “There Is Only User Time, Not ByteDance Time,” he argued [ https://substack.com/redirect/d3ad0cf3-ad88-402b-b7da-52367dea2f27?j=eyJ1IjoiNnhha3A0In0.Jik053lvvE-Ofpv9cpN9G_-RUPbW_JKjUogath2oHFk ] that in a ruthlessly competitive market, users flock to products because they actually meet their needs. Cultural preferences, Mr Ming insisted, should not be weaponised into moral hierarchies. Reading a biography of a Song-dynasty poet is not inherently superior to binge-watching a micro-drama or scrolling through short videos. People vote with their thumbs, and their choices reflect genuine value. It is a robust defence. Yet to wave this away as mere consumer choice ignores the reality of the algorithmic age. A world-class recommendation engine does not merely cater to preexisting desires; by endlessly calibrating what users encounter next, it actively shapes them. It deliberately blurs the line between passively serving an audience and actively inducing its habits. In China, however, this universal debate over corporate responsibility intersects with a deeply ingrained official, cultural, and intellectual paternalism. There is a long-standing tradition among the country’s elites of delineating what constitutes “edifying” pursuits versus “vulgar” distractions. This top-down moralising begs an uncomfortable question: by what right must every hour of a citizen’s day be productive or edifying? If hundreds of millions of ordinary people, exhausted by the grinding pressures of a hyper-competitive society, actively seek out the empty solace of a three-minute soap opera, why should they be scolded for it? And what makes an opera sung in a foreign language, or some music show put up by instruments that most cannot even name, inherently more worthy than a melodrama on Hongguo? The right to be unproductive—and yes, even the right to consciously choose "vulgarity" and escapism—is a fundamental, if unacknowledged, human comfort. The phrase "ByteDance Time" is therefore a tribute to a commercial juggernaut masquerading as a regulatory red flag. The company should certainly answer for how its code shapes human habits. But it should not have to bear the burden of intellectually redeeming the masses. As cultural gatekeepers fret over what these hours mean for society, they forget whose time is actually being spent. The clock belongs to the audience. And whether an evening spent on Hongguo is culturally edifying or cheerfully wasted is a verdict that rests with no one else. (Enditem)

“How are you feeling about the election?” is the question I get from pretty much everyone I talk to this time of year. People who don’t know me as well usually follow up with some version of “sorry, you must get tired of talking about this.” But I don’t! I honestly love talking about politics with anyone, anytime for the same reason I do it professionally: I’m a sicko. I’ll be honest, though: outside of work, I’m less comfortable having conversations with people about their politics, especially if they differ from mine. Some of that is a congenital aversion to conflict that I promise I’m working on. But I feel like these conversations have become even harder. And I’m not the only one. Fifty-six percent of Americans have stopped discussing political news with someone because of something that person said, according to Pew. That’s up from 45% in 2024. Look, I get it. Speculating with someone about what other voters will do on Tuesday is easier than asking what they’re gonna do and why – especially if they give you an opinion you disagree with, or tell you they aren’t convinced voting is even worth it. At which point you might change the subject because you’d rather not pick a fight or just don’t know what to say next. The internet and social media have given us countless ways to express our opinions while avoiding conversations that ask more of us. We can post our takes (guilty), fight with strangers (guilty), share things we agree with and dunk on things we don’t (guilty and guilty). And we can do it all without ever having to really understand someone else’s frustration, or respond to criticism we find unfair, or actually try to persuade people who might need more than a link you posted with an all-caps THIS and multiple clap emojis. Campaigns have a related problem: they’ve gotten much better at figuring out which voters they want to reach, but much worse at actually reaching them. In 2024, Democrats made 300 million attempts to contact voters by phone. They got through just 3% of the time. And honestly, my first reaction was: who are those 3%? I barely answer calls from people I know. Same with texts and emails. That’s why we have spam filters and Ring cameras. We’ve spent years developing increasingly sophisticated ways to avoid strangers who want something from us – while campaigns have spent that time developing increasingly sophisticated ways to become the strangers we avoid.
