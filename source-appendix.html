A Letter to Myself on 9/11

THE ADVANTAGE—AND PERHAPS THE BURDEN—of looking back across a quarter century is knowing how little we understood then about how profoundly those few hours would change the country, the military, my family, and millions of other Americans. I wish I could tell that version of myself what I know now. I was one of a very few Americans whose 9/11 story began before September 11, 2001. In the late summer, I was among a group of newly selected generals and admirals at a classified lunch with CIA Director George Tenet. Asked about threats to the country, he described several but emphasized a terrorist organization called al Qaeda. I had little idea what al Qaeda was, but he made sure we remembered the name. That was around the same time I reported to the Pentagon for my first Washington assignment, as vice director for force development (J7) on the Joint Staff. Our section handled contingency war plans, exercises, and force transformation. Someone had described it as a relatively sleepy assignment. My boss, a Marine lieutenant general, knew that after several years training soldiers in the California desert, I had seen little of my extended family on the East Coast. He encouraged me to take a long weekend before jumping into the job. My wife and I left Washington on September 8, planning to return four days later. And then the phone rang while we were having a leisurely breakfast with my in-laws on eastern Long Island. My brother-in-law called to say that it appeared an airplane had just crashed into the World Trade Center. The United States was under attack. Soon, we would be at war. Twenty-five years later, I know things now that I could not possibly have known as I turned on the television just in time to see the second plane hit. This is the letter I would send to myself if I could, warning about what was to come. Mark, You don’t have much time to read this. You’re going to be busy for the next several years.
