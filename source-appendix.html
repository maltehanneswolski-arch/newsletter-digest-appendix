A Barbarous Cliché

Fang Xinghai is in serious trouble. On July 24, China’s anti-corruption authorities announced that the former vice-chairman of its securities regulator was under investigation for suspected “serious violations of discipline and law”. Three weeks later, The Wire China recalled another of his supposed roles: Mr Fang had been the party-state’s “best barbarian handler”. His qualifications were a Stanford education, excellent English, charm and an unusual ability to explain Chinese policy to foreign VIPs. The immediate trail leads to Sinocism, the most influential China newsletter. Its publisher called him on X a “top barbarian financier handler” on the day of the announcement; three days later Sinocism used “chief barbarian financier handler.” As far back as 2015, Sinocism described him as “a very good barbarian handler”. One wonders what title remains for the next Chinese official who speaks fluent English to foreigners. Oddly, this was already The Wire China’s second outing with the phrase this year. On February 1, it advertised an interview with Tsinghua economist David Daokui Li as “a discussion with barbarian-handler David Daokui Li”. The Wire China is a serious and useful publication, and these instances seem more like an unfortunate lapse in language than anything characteristic of its work. Nor is it alone. In 2024, the Wall Street Journal called Vice-Premier He Lifeng the latest in a “long line” of Chinese officials occupying a role “referred to in diplomatic circles as a ‘barbarian handler’”. It did more than merely repeat the term: the article devoted two consecutive paragraphs of its first five to entrenching the conceit, describing successive Chinese officials as “barbarian handlers” and tracing the supposedly “tongue-in-cheek” expression to a centuries-old Chinese suspicion and disdain for outsiders. In doing so, it turned a piece of diplomatic slang into an established historical category. The term is routinely presented as if it reveals something ancient and peculiar about China. Yet whenever it surfaces in contemporary usage, it is justified by some version of “referred to in diplomatic circles”—in other words, by the very Western officials, diplomats and journalists who use it. What looks like an inherited Chinese category is therefore a Western judgment dressed up as insider terminology. There is some history behind the joke, but less than its users imply. Imperial Chinese texts certainly used yi (夷) for outsiders. Yet the supposedly straightforward equation of yi with the English “barbarian” is itself historically contested. Dilip K. Basu challenged the entrenched rendering of yi (夷) as “barbarian”. Lydia H. Liu argued that the equivalence itself was forged in the 19th century Anglo-Chinese encounter: British translators rendered yi as “barbarian”, then used a treaty to prohibit the Chinese word while remaining free to call the Chinese barbarians themselves. Article 51 of the 1858 Treaty of Tientsin specifically prohibited Chinese official documents from applying yi (夷)/“barbarian” to Britain or its subjects. Now, it’s widespread in NYC for local physical infrastructure projects – usually around schools, libraries, streets – with the simple requirement that they cost at least $50,000 and will last at least 5 years. Districts hold neighborhood meetings to collect ideas for community improvement projects. Idea collection is focused particularly on hard-to-reach communities, such as immigrants with limited English proficiency, youth, senior citizens, and public housing residents. Volunteers then research community needs and work with city agencies to turn ideas into concrete proposals. As a resident, it’s a fun process. First, districts hold community meetings where residents brainstorm ideas and appoint volunteer delegates to shape them into proposals. Then, you get a description of a number of proposed projects in your neighborhood and get to choose, say, three of them that part of your community’s tax money will be spent on. Do you want to plant more trees on the streets? Build a lab for the school around the block? Buy an aquatic weed harvester for the local lake? While voting for your local politicians hopefully allows you to be indirectly involved in how the city spends your taxpayer money, it’s nice to feel like you are contributing directly to where some of it goes. One of the coolest (and most contributist!) things about participatory budgeting in New York City is that anyone can participate. Regardless of incarceration status, citizenship, or age (you can vote even as young as 11!). If you live and pay taxes in the community, you’re able to help direct where some of those taxes go.
