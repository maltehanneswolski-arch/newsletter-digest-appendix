Germany Vs the Holocaust

As far as most people in the West were concerned, John Demjanjuk (1920–2012) was a for decades one of the world’s most notorious Nazis. An Ukrainian-born former Soviet Red Army soldier, Demjanjuk was captured by German forces in 1942 and imprisoned in Nazi prisoner-of-war encampments, some of the deadliest places in WW2: since the Soviet Union hadn’t signed the Geneva Conventions, Germany didn’t feel itself bound by them. While Western prisoners were, by and large, relatively well treated, Soviet prisoners suffered most appalling conditions of incarceration. Over half of the around six million Soviet prisoners of war died in Nazi Germany. Demjanjuk chose to survive. When he was given the chance to leave by enrolling as an auxiliary guard for the Nazis, he jumped at it. Nazi Germany loved Ukrainian auxiliaries whom they could turn into anti-Soviet forces, and Demjanjuk loved to not die. Demjanjuk served in some capacity in camps holding Jewish and other inmates, particularly in Sobibor, an extermination camp in occupied Poland in 1943. After the war, he migrated to the United States, settling in Ohio and working as an auto mechanic. He became an US citizen in 1958. This man’s American Dream ended abruptly in the 1970s, when American investigators suspected he was “Ivan the Terrible,” a brutal Treblinka camp guard infamous for operating gas chambers and committing savage violence against prisoners. Such was the start of Demjanjuk’s legal tribulations. In 1981, the United States revoked his citizenship, and he was extradited to Israel to stand trial for crimes against humanity. The Israeli trial became a global media circus. Several survivors identified Demjanjuk as Ivan the Terrible and, in 1988, he was sentenced to death. By this time, he had already spent some fifteen years as an alleged Nazi monster, and his children had spent all that time reading about their father was perhaps the most despicable person alive.
