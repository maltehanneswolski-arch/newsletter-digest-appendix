Europe-China Trade Negotiations: Can Brussels Face Reality? – Part 1

The European Union encounters serious and ongoing problems when it comes to negotiating trade and economic agreements with China. These difficulties extend well beyond issues such as tariffs, quotas or access to markets. Structural economic differences, political fragmentation within Europe and the wider geopolitical tensions are revealing a more fundamental issue: Brussels continues to lack a proper understanding of the China it is negotiating with and of the world in which it now has to compete. The Strategic Disconnect: Europe’s China Blindspot The fact that European negotiators frequently fail to take into account China’s long-standing civilizational approach to statecraft and its historical memory shows that there is a major cultural and conceptual mismatch. Although economic and regulatory differences cause immediate tension, a deeper lack of awareness of history, and of political and economic philosophy results in a continuing strategic misalignment. It’s not just a matter of tariffs or quotas; it’s a reflection of Europe’s short-term memory compared to China’s long-term strategy. Brussels is still governed by a mainly technocratic and transactional approach, whereas Beijing conducts its negotiations with the kind of patience typical of a civilization that thinks in terms of decades and centuries rather than election cycles. The European Union’s habit of looking at China mostly from regulatory, economic and ideological points of view prevents it from seeing the fundamental causes of tension. China is not merely another trading partner that can be forced to alter its economic model in accordance with European preferences. It is a major power which has its own historical experience, political system, economic model and strategic goals. Europe needn’t approve of China’s political system, but if it wishes to negotiate effectively then it must understand it. EU leaders ought to remember what the former Singaporean diplomat Kishore Mahbubani has so clearly stated: “China is the country which will drive the 21st century – that should be clear to all.” The issue is that Brussels too frequently deals with China from a stance of assumed superiority. Although the argument that “China is not a democracy” is constantly employed in order to set Europe apart from Beijing, it fails to shed any light on how China actually thinks or why its policies have led to the economic results they have. As Kishore Mahbubani has argued, it is difficult to transfer political values from one civilisation to another. In China, historical experience has given particular weight to political stability, social order, peace and material well-being, while Western-style liberal democracy is not necessarily regarded as the overriding political priority. Mahbubani points to surveys showing exceptionally high levels of satisfaction with China’s government, while arguing that many Chinese associate political stability, effective governance and economic well-being more closely with legitimate government than with Western-style liberal democracy. Harvard Kennedy School surveys have repeatedly found more than 90% satisfaction with the Chinese government in direct-question surveys in China. Ignoring these differences does not cause them to vanish. It only makes European strategy less effective.
