Liquidate Your Moral Debts

I’m not here to give you life advice, so I won’t. But let me say that moral debts are dangerous things to hold. It’s not that moral debts (unlike actual money debts) are worth nothing. It’s worse than that: moral debts are dangerous for the lender, for the person actually making the effort of doing something for somebody else. This is one of those lessons that one sees constantly being rediscovered in history. Remember what happened to Julius Caesar when he spared all those enemies he had made, and let them live in his moral debt? Remember how much they enjoyed being pardoned by the great Caesar ? On his return to Rome, Caesar faced the difficult task of reconciliating former enemies and the families or the slain. Unlike Sulla and Marius, he generally preferred pardons and understandings to proscriptions and executions. Prominent men in the Pompeian camp, like Marcus Junius Brutus – long suspected of being Caesar’s natural son by an upper-class married woman – Cassius and Cicero himself returned to public life with no penalties. In some cases, Caesar’s forbearance was remarkable: in December, 45 BC, Caesar was amicably meeting Cicero in the writer’s sumptuous Puteoli villa, having an expensive dinner with a man who had been his sworn enemy (and secretly remained so). Quintus Ligarius, already pardoned in Pharsalus, had again joined his enemies in Africa, and was pardoned once more; Plutarch writes that he never forgave Caesar for that. Of course, Quintus Ligarius was one of Caesar’s murderers. This lesson has never changed throughout history. William Makepeace Thackeray knew it, and wrote thus in “Vanity Fair,” (1848) chapter XVIII: “When one man has been under very remarkable obligations to another, with whom he subsequently quarrels, a common sense of decency, as it were, makes of the former a much severer enemy than a mere stranger would be. To account for your own hard-heartedness and ingratitude in such a case, you are bound to prove the other party’s crime. It is not that you are selfish, brutal, and angry at the failure of a speculation—no, no—it is that your partner has led you into it by the basest treachery and with the most sinister motives. From a mere sense of consistency, a persecutor is bound to show that the fallen man is a villain—otherwise he, the persecutor, is a wretch himself. And as a general rule, which may make all creditors who are inclined to be severe pretty comfortable in their minds, no men embarrassed are altogether honest, very likely. They conceal something; they exaggerate chances of good luck; hide away the real state of affairs; say that things are flourishing when they are hopeless, keep a smiling face (a dreary smile it is) upon the verge of bankruptcy—are ready to lay hold of any pretext for delay or of any money, so as to stave off the inevitable ruin a few days longer. “Down with such dishonesty,” says the creditor in triumph, and reviles his sinking enemy. “You fool, why do you catch at a straw?” calm good sense says to the man that is drowning. “You villain, why do you shrink from plunging into the irretrievable Gazette?” says prosperity to the poor devil battling in that black gulf. Who has not remarked the readiness with which the closest of friends and honestest of men suspect and accuse each other of cheating when they fall out on money matters? Everybody does it. For years, Yan Xuetong, one of China’s best-known international relations scholars, has advanced a striking argument: changes in world politics cannot be understood through structural forces alone. In his framework of “moral realism,” political leadership is a key variable. Material power and a country’s place in the international system set broad constraints, but leaders help determine how states act within them. The idea runs through his 2011 book Ancient Chinese Thought, Modern Chinese Power , his 2019 Leadership and the Rise of Great Powers , and the 2023 volume The Essence of Interstate Leadership: Debating Moral Realism In a 2026 essay , Yan put the point particularly clearly: because a great power’s relative position is comparatively stable, political leadership is the theory’s “core independent variable.” The essay below brings that argument directly into the present. Looking at the 2026 Iran war, Yan asks why conflict broke out when it did, given that geography, regime differences and the US-Israeli military advantage had existed for years.
