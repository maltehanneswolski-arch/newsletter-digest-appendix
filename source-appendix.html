Why English keeps returning to China’s culture wars

A familiar argument has returned to the Chinese internet. Tang Jiafeng, a popular maths tutor, has called for English to be downgraded in schools, arguing that most Chinese will have little use for it after graduation and that too much time is spent learning it. What made the intervention more combustible was his claim that the era of Chinese people “worshipping foreign things” ought to end. Hu Xijin, the former editor of the Global Times, pushed back Xiang Ligang, a telecoms commentator with nationalist leanings, challenged Hu in turn, extending the exchange into a three-day series of responses from Hu. There is some irony in seeing Mr Hu, long portrayed abroad as a prominent voice and promoter of Chinese nationalism, now arguing against one of its more inward-looking strains. The argument is familiar because versions of it surface every few years. Sometimes the complaint is about English’s weight in the gaokao. Sometimes it is about years spent memorising vocabulary that students may never use. Sometimes it becomes a cultural grievance: why should Chinese children work so hard at English when so few Western children learn Chinese? China’s English teaching is often inefficient, and there is nothing unreasonable about debating how much time schools should devote to it. But the regularity with which the argument returns suggests that something else is bothering people. For much of the reform era, English was part of the price of admission to a world from which China wanted knowledge, technology, investment and opportunity. Scientists needed it to read and publish internationally; officials and scholars used it to deal with foreign counterparts; companies needed it as they moved abroad. Millions of Chinese students spent years learning a language that Americans and Britons acquired without effort. That asymmetry was real, and easier to accept when China was plainly poorer and trying to catch up. It feels different when the country doing the learning is the world’s second-largest economy, dominates large parts of global manufacturing and increasingly speaks of civilisational confidence. The old arrangement can begin to look less like practicality and more like hierarchy. Why, some Chinese ask, should their children still spend so much effort mastering the language of countries China has already surpassed in some fields? That is where a sensible argument about education can turn into one about national dignity. Complaining that English is badly taught is not the same as believing it should matter less. Nor is criticising exam-heavy instruction the same as treating the language itself as a residue of cultural submission. The phrase “worshipping foreign things” matters because it shifts the question from usefulness to loyalty. Yet irritation with a linguistic hierarchy does not make it disappear. English remains useful because too much of the world already operates through it. Its position was built on Anglo-American power, but it now rests just as much on accumulated habit, institutions and network effects. China may reasonably expect its own rise to make Chinese more valuable internationally. But that process will depend on foreigners having stronger reasons to learn Chinese, not on Chinese people deciding in advance that English ought to matter less.
